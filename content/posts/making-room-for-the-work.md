For years, I waited for the right conditions to make anything.

The clear desk. The open afternoon. The feeling of being ready. I treated creativity like a guest who needed fresh flowers and a clean house before she would consider stopping by.

She was not stopping by.

## The myth of the big empty afternoon

Somewhere along the way, I decided real work happened in large, uninterrupted blocks. A whole free morning, ideally. A day without meetings. A magical stretch of time with no laundry to fold and no email to answer.

That kind of afternoon does happen, occasionally. It just isn’t the kind of afternoon most of us can count on.

What we can count on is twenty minutes before lunch. A paragraph after dinner. A quick sketch between the things already on the calendar. Small moments that feel almost too small to matter — until they’re not.

I started leaving my notebook open on the table. Not tidying the whole desk, just moving the mug and finding a pencil. The invitation wasn’t to finish anything. It was to begin.

## Start before you’re ready

The work doesn’t need a ceremony. It needs a door left a little open.

Here are a few small things that have helped me keep that door open:

- Leave your tools somewhere you’ll see them.
- Decide on a next step so you don’t have to decide everything.
- Give yourself a short timer, then stop when it rings.
- Let a rough first attempt be a real attempt.
- Notice when you’ve done enough for today.

None of these are revolutionary. That might be the point.

They make the distance between thinking about the work and actually touching it a little shorter. They turn making something into a thing you can do on a Tuesday, rather than a thing you have to become a whole different person to do.

## Small is still a shape

Last month, I wrote for fifteen minutes most mornings. Not every morning. Not especially well. But by the end, there was something on the page I could read, revise, and make better.

It was not a grand gesture. It was a quiet accumulation. One pencil mark after another.

The things we make don’t always need more ambition. Sometimes they need a little more permission to begin imperfectly, in the time we already have.
